# Why: the drivers behind Australia's economy, 2019–2026

*A synthesis of the driver-tree research in `reports/research/drivers_*.md` (each with primary sources) and the
`auseconomy` data model. Written 26 October 2026. Confidence tags: [H] high, [M] medium, [L] low. Each section runs
driver → sub-driver → why it was that way → who benefits from it staying that way → what would change it.*

---

## 1. Why inflation surged, and why it's back

**The shape.** 7.8% at the December 2022 peak; 2.1% by mid-2025; 3.9% headline / 3.6% underlying by June 2026.

### 1a. The 2021–23 surge: roughly 55% supply, 40% demand, 5% margins [M]

The RBA's three decomposition methods agree on the order of magnitude: supply shocks explain half to three-quarters
of the overshoot; without them, underlying inflation would still have been 3.1–3.5%. That residual is the demand story,
and it has a specific cause.

**Supply (≈55%)** [H]
- Import prices and shipping drove 2021; Treasury attributes ~60% of the 7.8% to pass-through of import prices and labour costs.
- Energy: wholesale gas went from ~$6 to $11/GJ and spiked to $40/GJ in May 2022 as coal units failed, mines flooded, and the Ukraine shock hit. Direct CPI effect only ~½pp, but it ran through every input chain.
- New dwellings +20.7% in a year: materials and labour shortages colliding with HomeBuilder demand pulled forward.
- Floods and weather pushed fruit and vegetables; food ran at ~9.6%.

**Demand (≈40%): fiscal over-compensation, then saved, then spent** [H]
- COVID support totalled $429b (JobKeeper $90b, cash-flow boost $35b, JobSeeker supplements $29b, Stage 2 tax cuts brought forward $26b).
- Households lost $101b of income to COVID and received $110b more than that back: government paid about **$2 for every $1 lost** (Murphy, ANU; cited by the RBA Review).
- Consumption was forcibly suppressed, so the excess became ~$260–300b of saving (20% of a year's disposable income), then was spent into an economy at 3.5% unemployment.
- Monetary policy amplified it: cash rate 0.1%, "no rise until 2024" guidance, $281b of bond purchases, $188b Term Funding Facility. Murphy's estimate: policy stimulus added 3.0pp to 2022 inflation, 2.4pp fiscal and 0.6pp monetary.
- Dwelling prices +22% in 2021 added a wealth effect on top.

**Margins (≈5%)** [H]: non-mining profit shares were flat; the Australia Institute's "69% profits" figure is a GDP-deflator artefact dominated by mining exports. Supermarkets and energy retailers widened margins at the edges; they did not cause the inflation.

**Why it was that way.** In March 2020 nobody knew the downside, so support was sized for a depression that didn't arrive and kept flowing through 2021 for political and administrative reasons (JobKeeper's second phase, HomeBuilder extensions). The RBA's own review calls the 2020 shift to waiting for actual rather than forecast inflation "unwise" and the forward guidance credibility-damaging; it was two to three quarters late in 2022. **Who benefited:** asset owners (every asset class was repriced by the 0.1% rate), firms that received JobKeeper while profitable, and homeowners who fixed at 2%. **Who paid:** wage earners, through a 4–5% permanent real pay cut, and renters.

### 1b. The 2025–26 re-acceleration: half capacity, half one-offs [M]

Headline rose 1.8pp from mid-2025 to mid-2026. About 0.9pp is underlying (services at 3.9%, new dwellings 5.3% as builders removed discounts, health insurance), ~0.5pp is the electricity rebate expiring, and the rest is fuel and the Middle East conflict. Underneath: unit labour costs grew 5% through 2025 with productivity at zero, the RBA cut rates three times in 2025 into an economy with a positive output gap, and public demand hit a record 28.9% of GDP. The RBA has since reversed: 3.60% → 4.60% by September 2026.

**On "is government spending to blame":** across every Commonwealth channel the model can measure, the direct link from spending growth to underlying inflation is small and vanishes when 2020-21 is excluded. But that is not the whole answer. The *level* of public demand at a record share of GDP, rebate cycles that whipsaw the CPI (−0.6pp then +0.6pp), and care-sector wage decisions funded by government all add to the capacity pressure the RBA is now leaning against. Fiscal policy didn't light the fire; since 2024 it has been pouring on fuel at the margin while monetary policy tries to put it out.

**What would change it:** keep the fiscal impulse neutral or contractionary while inflation is above band; stop rebate cycles; supply measures where non-tradables are sticky (housing, energy, construction).

## 2. Why migration surged, and whether it was necessary

**The shape.** Forecast 235k; actual 538k (2022-23), 429k (2023-24), 302k (2024-25).

### 2a. Decomposition [H]
- Against the 2014-19 norm, 72% of the 2022-23 excess was extra arrivals, 28% was people not leaving.
- Students +262k (pre-COVID peak ~107k), visitors converting onshore +90k, working holiday makers +71k, temporary skilled +44k. Permanent migration (+60k) was *below* its historical average.
- Most of the surge refilled the pandemic hole: cumulatively, by 2023-24 NOM was only 85k (5.7%) above the pre-COVID path. The problem was speed, not total.

### 2b. The decisions, and what each did [M]
| Decision | When | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Student work-hour cap removed | Jan 2022 (Morrison), extended to Jun 2023 (Labor) | Drew work-motivated applicants; student participation 44% → 61%; tens of thousands |
| Pandemic Event visa kept open | to Sep 2023 | Stock 10k → 122k; cut departures |
| Processing surge, backlog 1m → 755k | late 2022 | Pulled 50–100k arrivals forward into one year |
| Post-study work rights +2 years | Jul 2023 (unwound Jul 2024) | Graduate visa stock 99k → 278k; delays departures for years |
| Permanent program 160k → 195k | Jobs & Skills Summit, Sep 2022 | ≤15k: two-thirds of places went to people already onshore |
| "Weakened" Genuine Temporary Entrant test | not supported by evidence | Grant rates were *below* historical levels; volume, not leniency |

**Was it necessary?** Shortages were real: vacancies hit 480k in May 2022, led by health, hospitality, professional services and construction. But the inflow didn't target them: students worked disproportionately in hospitality and retail, and the permanent uplift relabelled people already here. Universities' dependence ($10.2b of international fees, 25% of sector revenue, >40% at some institutions) and a $50b education export line made the volume politically protected. Every lever eventually used (work-hour cap, onshore switching bans, graduate visa limits, processing priority) was available in early 2023, when the Parkinson and Nixon reviews reported.

**Verdict [M]:** 55–65% mechanical reopening catch-up; 15–25% unintended consequences of specific settings; 10–15% deliberate shortage policy; 5–15% institutional incentives. **The lasting legacy is the onshore stock, not the peak:** 446k bridging visas (177k in 2023), 278k temporary graduates, 551k students. That stock is why the Budget's 245k → 225k path is optimistic (the agent's call: 250–290k for 2026-27) and why departures, not arrivals, are now the thing to watch.

### 2c. Granular impacts
- **Rents:** +7.8% y/y at the 2024 peak; vacancy 1.4%. The RBA's rule of thumb (0.5% on rents per 100k students) applied to a ~500k excess gives 4–5%; our regressions give more but don't control for supply. Note that rents started rising in 2021, before borders reopened, because households got smaller (2.55 → 2.48 people, ~120k extra households). [H direction, M size]
- **Where:** Sydney and Melbourne took 58% of 2022-23 NOM.
- **Housing arithmetic:** 2024 net supply 155k vs underlying demand 223k.
- **Wages:** no measured effect on incumbent wages; labour composition *added* ~0.3pp a year to productivity. [established]
- **GDP per capita:** fell seven quarters straight to Sep 2024 while GDP grew. Arithmetic, not causation; the ABS also later found population had been overstated by ~80k.
- **Exploitation:** 75% of students use agents; universities paid ~$250m a year in agent commissions; protection-visa decisions averaged 841 days; "visa hopping" rose from 2.5% to over 25% of graduate cohorts. This is the part of the system that was genuinely broken.

**Who benefited:** universities and private colleges, employers in hospitality and care, landlords, migration agents, and the Budget (students pay fees and taxes and use few services). **Who paid:** renters in Sydney and Melbourne, and the migrants exploited inside the system.

## 3. Why housing broke

### 3a. Supply can't respond [H]
- Completions 219k (2018) → 173k (2025) despite record prices. Completions per 1,000 people are at early-1980s lows.
- **Construction productivity:** dwellings per hour worked −53% over 30 years (−12% quality-adjusted). Why: the most fragmented industry in the economy (top-4 share 12%, average builder 1.5 employees); building-code changes adopted despite negative cost-benefit assessments and implemented differently by each state; apprenticeships stagnant; the Opal/Mascot defects shock to apartment confidence; fixed-price contracts meeting 17% input inflation → 3,000–3,600 construction insolvencies a year.
- **Zoning:** the RBA puts the zoning premium at ~42% of a Sydney detached house price. NSW's own Productivity Commission says planning rules add >50% to apartment costs. Grattan: allow three storeys everywhere and prices and rents end up 7–12% lower over a decade.
- **Taxes and charges:** industry estimates (caveat: industry-commissioned) put ~49% of a Sydney house-and-land package in taxes, charges and regulatory cost.
- **Feasibility:** a Sydney mid-rise apartment cost $666k to build in 2018 and $1.05m in 2025; development stacks up only east of Parramatta. Two rate rises in 2026 made it worse.
- **Government building:** the Housing Australia Future Fund delivered 1,432 of 40,000 homes by May 2026; public housing stock *fell* in 2024-25.

### 3b. Demand shocks were large but temporary [H]
- Interest rates: 0.1% cash rate and the $188b TFF produced +22% in 2021 alone. The RBA's own estimate: a sustained 1pp cut in real rates raises prices ~28% long run. Rate cuts in 2025 drove that year's rise too.
- Population: see §2. Plus smaller households post-COVID.
- Investor tax settings: ~1–2% on the *level* of prices (Grattan ~1%), not much of the 2019–26 change. The 2026 reforms are good policy with small price effects.
- First-home-buyer subsidies and stamp-duty concessions (>$1.6b a year by states) add demand exactly where supply is least elastic.

### 3c. Verdict (agent synthesis) [M]
| Driver | Price rise | Rent rise | Reversible in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rates and credit | 30–40% | 5–10% | 1 year |
| Zoning/planning (the multiplier) | 20–30% | 20–30% | 5–10 years |
| Population + household size | 15–25% | 35–50% | 1 year |
| Cost/productivity/insolvency shock | 10–15% | 10–20% | 5–10 years |
| Taxes and charges on new housing | 5–10% | 5% | 5–10 years |
| NG/CGT, FHB subsidies, foreign buyers, short-stays | <5% each | <5% each | 1 year, small |

**Why it stays this way:** states depend on stamp duty (>20% of revenue) so won't switch to land tax; councils answer to incumbents who capture amenity and bear none of the cost of exclusion; the Commonwealth controls migration, rates and tax but has no planning power, so it pays for outcomes (slowly) instead; the industry's risk sits on thinly capitalised small builders. Every actor is behaving rationally and the system produces 173k homes a year.

## 4. Why government spending grew, and whether it was responsible

**The shape.** Payments 24.4% of GDP (2018-19) → 26.8% (2025-26), the highest outside COVID since 1986. Since 2020, outcomes have beaten forecasts by 10% one year out and 17% three years out; before COVID forecasts were unbiased. Of $143b added to payments since the 2023-24 Budget, 52% was new decisions, 48% parameter changes.

### 4a. Where the growth came from, 2019-20 → 2025-26 ($b) and how much was chosen [M]
| Program | Then → now → 2029-30 | Price + demographics explain | Chosen |
|---|---|---|---|
| Disability (NDIS + DSP + carers) | 49 → 93 → 103 | ~40% | design failure (below) |
| Aged care | 20 → 41 → 51 | ~50% | Royal Commission response ($17.7b), FWC wage cases ($17.7b) |
| Hospitals (to states) | 23 → 34 → 47 | ~70% | $25b NHRA deal Jan 2026 (cost-shift from states) |
| Medicare | 25 → 35 → 42 | ~90% | $8.5b bulk-billing package |
| PBS | 13 → 22 → 24 | ~55% | new listings, co-payment cuts (flat forward path not credible) |
| Childcare | 8 → 16 → 21 | ~35% | Cheaper Child Care, 15% wage subsidy, 3-Day Guarantee |
| Defence | 33 → 53 → 62 | 0% | NDS/AUKUS, back-loaded |
| Debt interest | 17 → 28 → 47 | 0% | consequence of deficits × 4.8% borrowing cost |
| Fuel and energy (incl. rebates) | 8 → 20 → 17 | – | $6.8b of mostly untargeted rebates |

### 4b. The NDIS: grown beyond its intent [H]
- Designed (PC 2011) for 411,000 people with permanent and significant disability at $13.6b (~1% of GDP), with a second tier of lower-level supports *outside* the scheme. Reality: 782,000 people, $51.2b (1.7% of GDP), 10.5% growth last year; 11% of six-year-olds are participants; only 4% of disability funding sits outside the scheme because states withdrew when the Commonwealth started paying.
- Against the 2017 projection, spending is ~$16b a year above plan; against the original scope, $12–20b.
- **Why:** an uncapped, individualised, non-means-tested entitlement in which the Commonwealth pays every marginal dollar (states fixed at 4% growth), prices anchored at caps (>90% of therapy claims within 5% of the cap), 92% of plan-managed providers unregistered, and for a decade prevention controls that reviewed 0.4% of dollars. The NDIA's own leakage estimate is 8% (~$3.7b a year), of which ~1.3% is "likely serious offending": fraud is real, but design did most of the damage.
- **Decomposition of the $23b growth since 2021-22 (agent estimate):** genuine need 19–28%; eligibility drift 15–24%; wage indexation 27%; real plan inflation and over-use 30%; serious fraud 1–6% (inside the others). Roughly a third to a half of the growth is design failure, over-servicing or fraud.
- **The 2026 reforms:** cuts of 50% to community-participation and 10% to daily-living budgets from 1 October 2026; a functional-capacity test from 2028; participants projected 783k → 632k by 2030. Only ~10% of the $37.8b "saving" is integrity measures; 35% is the blunt budget cut. The Budget assumes ~2% growth for three years after a decade averaging 15–19%. The PBO: if growth is 10% instead, that alone prevents the 2030s surplus.
- **Who benefited:** providers (a $50b market with price caps that became price floors), plan managers ($166m a quarter in fees), the states (shielded by the 4% rule), and families for whom the scheme was the only functioning door. **Who paid:** taxpayers, and the people with severe disability whose scheme is now being cut across the board to fix a problem they didn't cause.

### 4c. Everything else
- **Aged care and childcare:** about half genuinely demographic; the rest were decisions a Royal Commission and the Fair Work Commission made close to unavoidable (care wages +15–28%). Defensible, but they lock in 5–7% nominal growth.
- **Hospitals:** a $25b transfer from states to Commonwealth with no activity or outcome conditions. Poor value as designed. [M]
- **Bulk billing:** the government's own estimate is $859m a year in patient savings against ~$2b a year in cost; the balance accrues to practices. [M]
- **Defence:** 26% real growth, back-loaded, and the only big line with no demographic driver. A choice.
- **Interest:** $17b → $47b. The silent cost of running structural deficits (1.6% of GDP, Treasury) into a 4.8% borrowing rate.
- **Energy rebates:** $6.8b that moved bills across years rather than lowering them: the Ausgrid household's net bill rose 26% in two years as rebates ended while the regulated price *fell*.

**Why spending is hard to control:** the big lines are demand-driven entitlements with no cap, indexed to wages or prices, where the Commonwealth pays and someone else (states, providers, courts) decides. Add a forecast convention that books "savings" against a projection rather than last year, and the political incentive to announce cuts that never arrive is built in.

## 5. Why productivity is weak and businesses don't invest

**The shape.** GDP per hour +1.1% a year in the 2010s, +0.2% since 2019. Market-sector productivity growth fell 1.5% → 0.5%; by growth accounting ~0.7pp of that is less capital per worker, ~0.3pp weaker multifactor productivity. The care-economy composition story explains ~8% of the slowdown; mining's grade decline is a large within-industry drag (−0.8pp) that is geology, not policy. Two caveats: accounting shares aren't causes (the RBA argues shallow capital is partly a *symptom* of weak demand and MFP), and migration's skill mix *added* to productivity.

### 5a. Why businesses don't invest, in order of evidential weight [M]
1. **Mining unwind (mechanical):** investment 17% → 11% of GDP as the LNG build ended; nothing replaced it. Non-mining investment is separately ~3pp of GDP below pre-GFC.
2. **Weak demand and declining competition:** markups +5% since the mid-2000s (+12% in digital sectors); new-firm entry 13% → 11%; job mobility at record lows; investment fell *most* among productive firms in sectors where markups rose. Incumbents don't need to invest to hold share; challengers can't get in. Merger law was weak until January 2026.
3. **Hurdle rates stuck at 12–15% while the cost of capital fell to ~6%.** Boards use round-number hurdles and 3–5-year paybacks that haven't moved in 20 years; managers carry the career risk of failure and none of the upside of the marginal project.
4. **Tax rewards paying out over reinvesting:** imputation makes shareholders (and super funds) want franked dividends; Australian payout ratios are among the world's highest; the 30% rate is above the OECD average but a cut mostly benefits foreign investors under imputation, so it's politically dead. Instant write-offs in the 2010s and COVID did not lift investment. The PC's cash-flow tax (modelled +2.2% investment) was not adopted.
5. **Capital and talent go to housing:** housing 54% → 65% of credit (2008–21). Banks earn risk-free returns on land; households earn tax-free leveraged returns on it; a founder's $200k becomes a deposit.
6. **The environment is second-order on the evidence:** energy is <2% of costs even for gas-intensive manufacturers; approvals delays bite in energy and infrastructure (>500 days); regulatory fragmentation and licensing (~20% of workers) are documented drags. Real, but smaller than items 1–4 in the aggregate data.
7. **Firm structure:** top 20% of firms do 80% of investment; Australia has few large non-resource firms and low business R&D (~0.9% of GDP vs ~1.9% OECD).
8. **What's turning:** data-centre capex (+77% y/y), narrow and import-heavy.

### 5b. Who decides and why it persists
Boards reward payout discipline; shareholders want franking; banks prefer mortgages; incumbents prefer not competing; governments prefer write-offs that don't work to cash-flow taxes and competition enforcement that create losers; households watch and buy houses. The economy got very good at owning things and stopped getting better at making them.

## 6. Why the tax system pushes young people away from productive assets

For a 30-year-old at the 32% marginal rate, $50k for ten years at 6% growth:

| | Effective tax on the return | Leverage |
|---|---|---|
| Shares, pre-2027 law | ~19% | little |
| Shares, from July 2027 (indexation + 30% minimum) | ~23% | little |
| Shares at the 47% rate | 34% → 39% | little |
| Own home | 0% | 10–20× with the government guarantee |

The home figures exclude interest, stamp duty and rent, so they're not a fair return comparison; the structure is the point. The main residence is untaxed on the way up and on sale, exempt from the pension assets test, and guaranteed to 95% leverage by the Commonwealth. The 2026 reform raised tax on shares for every bracket (a ~20% relative increase at the 30% rate) and left the family home untouched, so it narrowed the wage/capital gap and *widened* the gap between the asset young people can't afford and the ones they can. Franking still pulls young investors toward mature dividend payers rather than growth firms; super at 15% is locked until 60.

**Why it's built this way:** main-residence exemption (1985), negative gearing (1987), imputation (1987), the 50% discount replacing indexation (1999), super concessions: each solved a problem of its decade, each created a constituency, and by 2019 the median voter was an older homeowner who voted against touching any of it. The 2026 package went after investors and trusts and left the home alone because that's the coalition that can be beaten. Rational politics, poor allocation.

### Founders [M]
There is a visible set of anecdotes, not a measured exodus. Finder's Fred Schebesta moved to Singapore in October 2026 citing the CGT change (Finder, founded 2006, is too old for the proposed carve-out); Canva, Atlassian and Airwallex's moves were legal redomiciles or HQ shifts for US capital and market access, with staff and IP mostly staying. Aggregate departures of citizens are *below* the 2014-19 average; Henley's "millionaire migration" numbers were discredited. The real driver is market size and US capital; tax matters at exit. The 2026 change takes a $5m founder exit from 23.5% to 47% tax, but a draft Innovative Business CGT Concession (September 2026) would keep the 50% discount with no minimum for companies under 15 years old and $50m turnover. Australia's exit tax was already above the US's QSBS (0% to US$15m) and Singapore (0%); the carve-out restores the old position rather than beating peers. Cheap fixes: legislate it with simple safe-harbours, rollover relief for flips, and start measuring founder emigration at all.

## 7. What the drivers imply about what to do

Ranked by evidence and effect:
1. **Supply where it binds:** housing construction productivity and zoning; energy build-out on time; approvals that work in practice. These fix inflation's sticky core, housing, and productivity at once, and take 5–10 years.
2. **Stop the demand mistakes:** no more untargeted rebates; no new first-home-buyer subsidies; keep the fiscal impulse neutral while inflation is above band.
3. **Fix the budget's design flaws, not just its totals:** cap and co-fund the NDIS with the states properly; put outcome conditions on hospital money; publish bracket-creep revenue and forecast-vs-outcome reconciliations every Budget.
4. **Tilt the tax system toward building:** index thresholds; legislate the founder concession; move toward expensing or a cash-flow tax; make the family home carry some of the weight over time (land tax for stamp duty, assets-test treatment).
5. **Competition with teeth:** merger enforcement, non-compete ban, licensing reform.
6. **Migration: fix the stock, not the headline:** departures, visa chains, the agent economy, and student quality; keep the skilled permanent stream.
7. **Measure:** NDIS outcomes, a wealth survey, founder emigration, construction productivity. Every bad decision above was made in the dark.

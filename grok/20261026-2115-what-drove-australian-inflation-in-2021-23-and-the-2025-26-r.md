## 2. Agree / disagree

**2021–23 supply majority: agree on the direction, disagree on 55%.** RBA Bulletin, June 2023: to March 2023, Shapiro gives supply ≈3½pp and demand ≈2½pp; the other two methods leave inflation at 3.1–3.5% once supply is removed ([Bulletin](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/bulletin/2023/jun/pdf/estimating-the-relative-contributions-of-supply-and-demand-drivers-to-inflation-in-australia.pdf)). Peak CPI was 7.8% in the December quarter 2022 ([ABS](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/economy/price-indexes-and-inflation/consumer-price-index-australia/dec-quarter-2022)). Removing ~3½pp of supply from a ~7% rate leaves inflation only just above the midpoint. That is a larger supply share of the *overshoot* than 55%, and a smaller excess-demand share than 40%.

**Murphy’s 3.0pp (2.4 fiscal, 0.6 monetary) and “$2 per $1 lost”: agree that the paper says this** ([Economic Papers, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/1759-3441.12382)). Disagree that it can be added to the RBA shares. His COVID-only scenario peaks at 4.2%; actual was 7.8%. The 3pp is one model’s policy counterfactual, overlapping the supply shocks, not a slice of the CPI.

**Treasury “60% labour plus imports”: agree** ([FOI 3376](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2023-07/foi-3376.pdf)). It is a cost split. Labour costs are how a tight labour market shows up. It does not corroborate a supply share.

**New dwellings: agree.** ABS, year to September quarter 2022: +20.7%, and +17.7% excluding the HomeBuilder unwind. The findings’ “+17.8%” in the contributor line is the ex-grant figure, rounded the wrong way, and it is not the headline series.

**Migration timing: agree.** NOM −85k in 2020–21, +204k in 2021–22 on the revised series, +518k in 2022–23 ([ABS](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/people/population/overseas-migration/2022-23-financial-year)). The first print for 2021–22 was +171k. Too late for the 2022 peak; relevant to 2023 rents.

**Savings buffer: the stock is right, the “saved then spent” mechanism is softer.** RBA cited more than $250bn, later about $300bn, roughly a fifth of disposable income ([SMH, 15 Mar 2023](https://www.smh.com.au/money/borrowing/the-rba-says-we-have-a-300b-savings-buffer-but-the-truth-is-not-so-simple-20230309-p5cqtf.html)). Holdings were skewed to older and higher-income households, and a large part went to mortgage buffers rather than the till.

**Profits: agree with the findings, not the Australia Institute.** Non-mining margins were not an independent CPI driver. The 69% figure does not survive contact with the household consumption basket.

**RBA late, guidance too strong: agree in substance.** Bond purchases were $281bn; the three-year yield target exit was disorderly; “not until 2024” guidance was a credibility hit. I did not re-verify the $25bn / $35–58bn loss arithmetic or the $8bn TFF figure in this pass.

**2025–26 table: mostly agree.** Latest RBA quarterly year-ended figures, updated 30 September 2026: June 2025 2.1 / 2.7; September 3.2 / **2.9**; December 3.6 / **3.3**; March 2026 4.1 / 3.5; June 3.9 / 3.6 ([RBA](https://www.rba.gov.au/inflation/measures-cpi.html)). The findings’ 3.0 and 3.4 are the pre-revision vintage (the February Statement still had December trimmed mean at 3.4). August monthly 4.0 / 3.6 is current ([ABS](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/economy/price-indexes-and-inflation/consumer-price-index-australia/sort)). Cash-rate path is right: 3.60% into 2026, then 3.85 (February), 4.10 (March), 4.35 (May), 4.60% from 30 September ([RBA](https://www.rba.gov.au/cash-rate-target-overview.html)).

**“Half genuine excess demand, arguably public”: disagree on the public half.** The RBA’s February 2026 chapter says capacity pressure “is unlikely to explain the majority” of the rise since mid-2025 ([SMP](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/smp/2026/feb/economic-conditions.html)). The same Statement says private demand, not public, was the surprise, and state rebate expiry was about 0.5pp of headline. March *monthly* CPI at 4.6%, with fuel +0.8pp, is not the quarterly 4.1% ([May SMP](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/smp/2026/may/overview.html)).

**RDP 2024-05 “±15pp”: over-precise.** Read’s paper finds sign restrictions often give *uninformative* sets for aggregate inflation ([RDP](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/2024/2024-05/)). That is a worse result for the Shapiro shares than a tidy ±15pp band, and the application is the United States.

## 3. What the findings miss (since mid-2026)

The annual national accounts landed on 23 October. In 2025–26, GDP rose 2.3% and GDP per capita 0.8%. GDP per hour worked rose **0.5%** (market sector +0.9%). Real unit labour costs rose **0.7%**, after +2.1% and +3.8% in the two previous years. Non-market gross value added slowed from 3.3% to 2.0%; the market sector accelerated from 1.3% to 2.5%. The household saving ratio rose from 5.5% to 6.1% ([ABS](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/economy/national-accounts/australian-system-national-accounts/latest-release)). That is the opposite of “zero productivity plus a fresh public-demand surge”.

Also missing: Hunter’s 25 October interview, above. Andrew Hauser on 21 October said the inflation outlook was “materially worse”, in part because of oil, and would not commit on November ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/australia-central-banker-says-policy-working-inflation-outlook-worse-2026-10-20/)). The 29 September decision already flagged higher energy prices and AI-related goods prices, and noted housing prices falling in most capitals ([decision](https://www.rba.gov.au/media-releases/2026/mr-26-27.html)). The wage price index was 3.2% in the year to the June quarter ([ABS](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/economy/price-indexes-and-inflation)). September-quarter CPI is still two days away (28 October). Markets are about 50–64% priced for 4.85% on 3 November; that is pricing, not data.

## 4. Weak points

The 55/40/5 verdict treats a shock decomposition, a cost decomposition and a policy counterfactual as slices of one pie. They overlap. Treasury’s labour share sits inside what the verdict calls demand; Murphy’s 3pp sits on top of the RBA’s supply residual. Adding them to 100% is the error.

“Public demand ≈28% of GDP” measures a level, mostly state-inclusive consumption and investment, and it has been high since about 2022. Transfers, which dominate the CIS 39% figure, do not enter public demand; they show up as private spending if they are spent. Inflation responds to growth relative to supply. In 2025–26 that growth was private, and the public sector slowed.

“Productivity ≈0” was a fair reading of the quarterly run of negative prints. It is not the 2025–26 year. Unit labour costs of 5.0–5.4% describe 2025; the annual real unit-labour-cost rise for 2025–26 is 0.7%, and the wage price index is 3.2%.

The rebate and fuel arithmetic is about right in total and easy to mis-time. Rebate expiry is a policy choice about *when* inflation prints, which the findings say. It is not evidence that the underlying rate was invented by the budget.

## 5. Verdict

The 2021–23 overshoot was chiefly global and domestic supply — goods, freight, fuel, energy, floods, materials — with a genuine domestic demand overshoot from fiscal over-compensation and a cash rate that stayed at 0.1% too long. Murphy is the right witness for “too much, for too long”. He is the wrong witness for a 40% share, and the Australia Institute is the wrong witness on profits.

The 2025–26 re-acceleration is roughly half underlying domestic pressure and half one-offs (rebate expiry, then oil). The domestic half is private demand — housing, durables, market services — running into an economy whose supply capacity was revised down, not a new blowout in public demand. A high *level* of government spending still matters: it uses scarce construction and labour capacity, which is why the IMF in October 2024 and Richardson in May 2026 are not wrong to watch the states and Canberra. It is not what turned 2.1% into 4%.

The levers that match the evidence are the ones the RBA is already pulling, plus not repeating rebate theatre, plus supply in construction and energy. A profits policy would miss the CPI. Confidence is medium-high that supply dominated 2021–23 and that public spending is being over-blamed for 2025–26; medium on the exact split, because the identifying restrictions do not deliver one.

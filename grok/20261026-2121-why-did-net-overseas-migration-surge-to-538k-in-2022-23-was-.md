## 2. Agree / disagree

**Levels. Agree.** Revised NOM is 538,340 in 2022–23 (738,410 arrivals, 200,070 departures) and 429,160 in 2023–24 (661,360 and 232,200). [ABS, 19 Dec 2025](https://www.abs.gov.au/media-centre/media-releases/annual-net-overseas-migration-falls-second-year-row). The first print was 518,000, with departures at 219,000. The pre-pandemic arrival average of 515,000 is in the [ABS 2023–24 release](https://abs.gov.au/statistics/people/population/overseas-migration/latest-release). March 2022 Budget Paper 3, Table A.5, had 180,000 for 2022–23 and 213,000 for 2023–24, then 235,000. The 235,000 figure is the October 2022 assumption. Excess over 2 × 235,000 is 497,000 by arithmetic. Excess over the findings’ own 227,000 pre-COVID average is larger.

**Visa nets. Partly.** Student *arrivals* were later put at 278,000 in 2022–23 and 207,000 in 2023–24 (ABS 2023–24 release). A net of +262,000 is an agent calculation. The ABS [warns](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/microdata-tablebuilder/available-microdata-tablebuilder/net-overseas-migration) that people leave on a different visa from the one they arrived on, so visa-nets are not stock changes. Students were the largest arrival group. That part holds.

**Catch-up. Snapshot only.** Summing ABS June NOM from 2018–19 to 2023–24 gives about 1.525 million. A flat 240,000 path produces an overshoot of about 85,000. A 227,000 path, which is the findings’ own average, produces a larger one. Adding 2024–25’s 305,570 takes a 240,000 path to roughly 150,000 above trend. “The hole was almost refilled” was a June 2024 statement.

**Decisions. Directionally agree, sizes are judgement.** Vacancies of 480,100 in May 2022, 111 per cent above February 2020, are [ABS](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/labour/jobs/job-vacancies-australia/may-2022). The permanent program lifts people already here onto permanent visas, so a 195,000 planning level cannot have added anything like 35,000 to NOM. The “≤15,000” and “tens of thousands” from work-hour settings are not in an official table. The 34.9 per cent offshore skill-grant share was not re-checked.

**Rents. The 7.8 per cent is real. The 4–5 per cent attribution fails.** CPI rents rose 7.8 per cent in the year to the March quarter 2024, the fastest since 2009, and 9.5 per cent excluding Commonwealth Rent Assistance ([ABS](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/economy/price-indexes-and-inflation/consumer-price-index-australia/mar-quarter-2024)). The RBA bulletin of [24 July 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-07-24/international-students-did-not-drive-rents-inflation-higher-rba/105562616) says 100,000 extra students, of whom about half rent, add about 50,000 renters and about 0.5 per cent to rents, and that this was a small share of the rise because advertised rents had already jumped before borders reopened. Applied as written, 0.5 per cent per 100,000 students is 2.5 per cent on a 500,000 flow, and that flow is not 500,000 extra students. Grattan’s [June 2024](https://grattan.edu.au/news/cutting-permanent-migration-may-make-housing-cheaper-but-it-will-definitely-make-us-poorer/) long-run result is smaller still: holding NOM at 160,000 permanently would leave rents about 6 per cent lower after a decade. Sydney 156,616 plus Melbourne 146,772 is 56 per cent of the revised 538,000, not 58 per cent ([ABS regional population, 2022–23](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/people/population/regional-population/2022-23)).

**Housing. Half-checked.** The National Housing Supply and Affordability Council on [21 May 2025](https://nhsac.gov.au/news/release-state-housing-system-report-2025) has 177,000 completions in 2024 against underlying demand of 223,000. “Net supply 155,000” and “172,000 completions in 2023” are not in that release.

**Wages and GDP per capita.** Seven quarterly falls in GDP per capita to the September quarter 2024 are [ABS, 4 Dec 2024](https://www.abs.gov.au/media-centre/media-releases/australian-economy-grew-03-cent-september-quarter). That is a denominator and productivity result sitting on top of rate rises. No study found here estimates the hit to native wages from the 2022–23 surge. e61’s June 2025 paper is the missing wage evidence, and it is about migrant graduates. An ABS population overstatement of about 80,000 was not found.

**225,000. The near-term number is on track. The later ones are a departure bet.** The [2026–27 Budget](https://budget.gov.au/content/overview/download/budget-overview-2026-27.pdf) has 295,000, 245,000, then 225,000. NOM was 292,100 in the year to March 2026, with 101,000 in the March quarter ([ABS, 17 Sep 2026](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/people/population/national-state-and-territory-population/sort)). The December 2025 Population Statement’s 260,000 for 2025–26 is already behind that run-rate ([Centre for Population](https://population.gov.au/publications/statements/2025-population-statement)). Setting a full-year departure assumption of 305,000 against year-to-March departures of 265,400 mixes periods. On Quantify’s reading of the same ABS release, departures in that year *fell* 2.2 per cent.

## 4. Weak points

The 55–65 per cent “mechanical catch-up” and the 15–25 per cent “unintended settings” count the same people twice. Low departures are why the catch-up did not reverse. The rent line then prices the 497,000 flow gap as if it were extra stock, after the catch-up test has put the stock gap near 85,000. The 85,000 itself only appears on a 240,000 path, not on the 227,000 average used for the arrival split. Visa-nets, the 80,000 population revision, and a year-to-March departure figure standing in for a financial-year forecast are measurement substitutions. The e61 hopper share is a 2009–2018 fact placed inside a 2022–23 story.

## 5. Verdict

The surge was a reopening of temporary visas, students first, into a labour market with 480,100 vacancies, on top of Coalition settings Labor kept and then added to: uncapped student work hours, the 408 visa, faster processing, and a two-year graduate extension. It was a poor match to the shortages, and the 195,000 permanent program barely moved NOM. It added something to rents in 2023–24 in Sydney and Melbourne while vacancies were on the floor. It does not explain most of the rent rise since 2020, and there is still no estimate of the effect on native wages. What remains is the onshore stock — bridging, graduate and review pipelines — which is why 225,000 is a hope about departures. Confidence is high on the accounts, medium on rents, and low on the percentage split of causes.

## 2. Agree / disagree

**Holds.**

* June 2026: 782,013 participants; $51.20b of supports in the year, up 10.5% from $46.35b ([NDIA monthly summary](https://dataresearch.ndis.gov.au/media/4541/download?attachment)). Scheme expenses $51.2b, also +10.5% ([Q4 quarterly report](https://www.ndis.gov.au/media/8795/download)).
* Disability sub-function outcome 2025–26 is $92.867b ([FBO appendix](https://archive.budget.gov.au/2025-26/fbo/download/05_appendix_a.pdf)). “11%” is a round of roughly 11.4% of the $812.1b expense estimate. The 2026–27 Budget has the sub-function at $98.143b in 2026–27 and $103.356b in 2029–30 ([BP1](https://budget.gov.au/content/bp1/download/bp1_2026-27.pdf)).
* $37.8b is the gross save versus the actuary’s unchanged-policy path over four years from 2026–27. Net of the estimates revision, Treasury’s figure versus the 2025–26 MYEFO is $24.6b over those four years, or $23.9b over five years from 2025–26. Cumulative cash reduction to 2036–37 is $184.9b, of which $152.4b is Commonwealth and $32.5b is lower state contributions ([Treasury FOI 4361](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-08/foi-4361-ir.pdf)). The $13.2b SCCP/daily-activities reset is Treasury’s table, reproduced by the [Parliamentary Library (14 Sep 2026)](https://www.aph.gov.au/About_Parliament/Parliamentary_departments/Parliamentary_Library/Research/Research_Note/2026-27/NDISExpenditureProjections) and then by Grattan.
* PBO, today: history averaged 18.7% a year over five years; the Budget path is under 2% in the forward estimates and about 5% after that; a 10% path adds $68.1b in 2036–37 and stops a surplus ([PBO](https://www.pbo.gov.au/publications-and-data/publications/2026-27-medium-term-budget-outlook)).
* Aged-care program expenses $41.4b to $50.7b by 2029–30. Hospital grants to the states, $33.9b to $47.2b, are +8.6% a year over those four years (same BP1). Commonwealth share of participant supports is 76% in 2025–26, then a floor of 73.4% ([Treasury FOI](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-08/foi-4361-ir.pdf)). States stay on a 4% escalator only until 30 June 2028, then actual growth capped at 8% ([HOA, Mar 2026](https://federation.gov.au/sites/default/files/about/agreements/hoa-nat-health-reform-agreement-ndis.pdf)).
* A “save” against a forecast is not a cut in last year’s spend. That point is the best thing in the note.

**Does not hold.**

* “Is it being cut? No.” The Amendment Act passed on 19 August 2026 ([Health](https://www.health.gov.au/securingtheNDIS)). From 1 October 2026, social, economic and community participation funding is cut 50% and improved daily living 10%. Treasury projects participants from 783,313 at 30 June 2026 to 632,379 at 30 June 2030, against a no-reform 895,358. Total NDIS program expenses dip from $56.1b in 2026–27 to $55.1b in 2027–28 (BP1). Participant-support dollars still edge up ($51.3b to $54.8b by 2029–30, fiscal balance, Treasury FOI). The aggregate line rising and the supports being cut are both true.
* “Slowed to a 5–6% target.” National Cabinet’s target is 5–6% ([PM, 30 Jan 2026](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/record-funding-public-hospitals-and-disability-reform)). The Budget path is about 0–5%, and 2025–26 still printed 10.5%. Grattan: the 5–6% track does not need the $13.2b blunt cut.
* “$13.9b of the $37.8b is the upward revision, so the real save is $23.9b.” Each number exists. They cover different windows (four-year gross, five-year parameter variation of $13.9b, five-year net versus MYEFO). Subtracting them is not an identity.
* “18.7% a year” from $27.9b to $51.2b. The PBO’s 18.7% is real. Treasury’s fiscal-balance series is $28.6b in 2021–22 to $51.3b in 2025–26, a 15.7% compound rate. The two facts do not describe one series.
* “Cost per participant rose 2.4% a year, so growth is mostly numbers, especially children.” The NDIA easy-read says the average payment rose 2.4% **over two years** ([easy-read Q4](https://ndis.gov.au/media/8806/download?attachment)). The latest year is $65,800 to $67,500 (+2.6%). The same participants went from $65,800 to $69,900 (+6%). Headcount rose 6% (739,414 to 782,013) against 10.5% spending growth. Children 0–8 are 162,781 people. Their average payments are far below the scheme average (AFSR: non-SIL autism about $28,300 and developmental delay about $15,700 in 2025–26, against a $67,500 scheme average). Children move the headcount. They do not move most of the dollars.
* “Almost no outcome measurement.” The NDIA publishes employment, social participation, carer employment, and choice and control. AIHW: 25% of NDIS participants in the labour force were in open employment at a full award wage in 2025–26 Q4, up from 20% in 2021–22 Q2 ([AIHW, updated 1 Oct 2026](https://www.aihw.gov.au/australias-disability-strategy/outcomes/employment-and-financial-security/ndis-participants-in-full-award-wage-employment)). What is missing is a dataset that can say whether $51b buys more than $40b. That narrower claim is fair.
* PBS “NDIS $42.3b in 2026–27”. BP1’s program expense, Commonwealth plus states, is **$56.1b**. Commonwealth participant supports are $39.7b (Treasury FOI). $42.3b is not the published program line.
* The $94.1b “2023–24 Budget” vintage for 2026–27, the DSP path to $40b by 2036–37, and the post-2020 forecast biases of 10/13/17% with a $73.6b/$69.3b policy/parameter split were not in the primary documents opened here. Paul Bloxham’s $145b “since taking office” ([AFR, 21 Oct 2026](https://www.afr.com/policy/economy/ndis-blowouts-lead-145b-surge-in-unexpected-spending-20261021-p617e2)) is a different window and is commentary.

## 3. What the note misses, since mid-2026

The Act is law, and the 1 October determination is in force. New assessments start 1 January 2028, not as a live lever now. Thriving Kids starts 1 October 2026 and is fully in place on 1 January 2028, later than first planned. The PBO’s 26 October outlook says the return to surplus is built on this NDIS save: a 5% miss delays it a year ($13.5b); 10% prevents it. e61 makes the same point ([e61](https://e61.in/hiding-in-plain-sight-the-big-item-that-underpins-the-budget/)). Plan inflation in the June 2026 quarter was 4.7%, down from 9.7% a year earlier, with intra-plan inflation 2.6% (quarterly report). The note’s “budget for 8%” sits next to the actuary’s pre-reform path of roughly 8–10% ([Health FOI 26-3289](https://www.health.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-09/foi-26-3289-budget-estimates.pdf)), which is a risk judgement, not the published target.

## 4. Weak points

The fraud question is ducked. John Dardo told the joint committee the statistically usable non-compliance rate is about 8.2–8.3% of claims, and that he cannot split organised fraud, opportunism and error ([Hansard](https://checkhansard.com.au/speech/5532054)). The 2,500 providers put on payment hold had claimed over $5b since the scheme began; he said that was not all fraud. The committee records about $4.8b on the same group ([committee, ch. 2](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/National_Disability_Insurance_Scheme/NDISIntegrity/Report/Chapter_2_-_Current_policy_landscape)). Prevented non-compliant payments are $1.2b from November 2022 to June 2026 ([NDIA, 23 Sep 2026](https://ndis.gov.au/news/11698-stronger-ndis-integrity-controls-save-billions)). Court recoveries are a few million. An 8% leakage band on a $51b scheme is serious. It is not 18% annual growth, and “$5b stolen this year” is a misquote.

The note also mixes three different “NDIS” numbers (participant supports, the BP1 program including states, and the disability sub-function with DSP and carers) inside single sentences, then reads a composition-depressed average payment as proof that price does not matter. Same-participant payments rose 6% last year.

## 5. Verdict

Growth was real, repeatedly above every vintage, and mostly more participants plus higher plans for people already in the scheme. Criminal fraud is a measured slice of claims in the high single digits, badly split between theft, over-servicing and error, and it is not why the scheme went from the high $20b range to $51b. Calling the 2026 package “not a cut, just 5–6%” is the wrong description of a law that is already reducing plan lines and is scored to shrink the future caseload. The honest fiscal statement is the one buried in the nuance: the headline save is against an actuary path the scheme has a habit of beating, and the PBO’s 10% case is the downside that matters. Confidence is high on the accounting, and only moderate on the fraud share, because the agency that pays the claims says it still cannot separate crime from error.
